The company behind hamlet

Impact in Business Recruitment Group Limited is the parent company behind hamlet, a connected commercial and residential property workspace developed for property professionals. Hamlet brings together workflows such as property discovery, portfolio and resource management, deal progression and professional communication, with a particular emphasis on making sustainability information more visible and useful in property workflows.

What the product is designed to do

Hamlet aims to reduce fragmentation across property workflows. It provides a digital environment in which brokers and other authorised users can organise opportunities, maintain deal context, coordinate next actions and engage with a professional community.

Commercial proposition

The company’s commercial opportunity is associated with developing, maintaining and distributing a specialist software platform and its related services. Product adoption, retention, willingness to pay, operating costs and the ability to execute are important variables to assess—not assumed outcomes.

Product differentiation

Hamlet’s positioning combines property workflow functionality with attention to energy performance and sustainability credentials, including standards such as Passivhaus and EnerPHit. Certification, ratings and property claims should always be checked against primary evidence.

Development priorities

As with any evolving software business, priorities include product reliability, security, privacy, accessible user experience, support, sustainable engineering practices and a credible route to long-term commercial viability.

How the open-source technology community works

Open source is both a development model and a community of practice. It can accelerate experimentation and make software inspectable, but it does not mean that every component is free of cost, risk or commercial constraint.

1. Source code and licences

An open-source licence grants permissions to use, inspect, modify and redistribute software under stated conditions. Permissive licences and copyleft licences have different obligations. A business should maintain an accurate software bill of materials and review licence compatibility before distribution or integration.

2. Public repositories and contribution

Projects commonly use repositories to host source code, documentation, issue trackers and proposed changes. Contributors may report defects, suggest features, submit pull requests, improve documentation or review code. Maintainers assess changes against project standards before acceptance.

3. Governance and maintainership

Healthy projects make decision-making understandable: who can approve changes, how releases are made, how disputes are handled and how project direction is set. Clear contribution guidance, a code of conduct and documented security-reporting channels support trust.

4. Community and reputation

Participation is earned through useful contributions, respectful collaboration, responsive issue handling and reliable releases. A community is not simply a marketing audience; contributors and users are independent participants whose interests and expectations must be respected.

5. Security and quality

Public visibility can help more people inspect software, but it does not guarantee that vulnerabilities will be found or fixed. Responsible maintenance includes dependency review, access controls, testing, release discipline, vulnerability response and clear communication of material incidents.

6. Commercial sustainability

Open-source projects still require funding for engineering, infrastructure, security, documentation, support and governance. Commercial models may include hosted services, enterprise features, support, implementation or complementary products, provided the model is consistent with the applicable licences.

For investors, repository activity is one source of technical evidence—not a proxy for revenue, customer demand, product-market fit or enterprise value. Assess the licence position, ownership and provenance of code, contributor arrangements, dependency exposure, release practices and the resources available to maintain critical components.

Maintaining a healthy investor relationship

A constructive investor relationship is built on consistency, accuracy, confidentiality and clear expectations. It should enable informed scrutiny without creating selective disclosure, unrealistic promises or an obligation to provide information that the company cannot properly share.

Establish a clear point of contact

Route enquiries through a designated Investor Relations mailbox, acknowledge receipt and identify the appropriate company representative for follow-up.

Set expectations early

Explain the information available, the expected response process and any limits on access to confidential, personal or commercially sensitive information.

Use reliable reporting

Share material updates in a consistent format, distinguish actual performance from targets or forecasts, explain assumptions and correct errors promptly.

Maintain a regular cadence

Where appropriate, agree a proportionate schedule for updates and meetings. Record questions, commitments, decisions and agreed next steps.

Protect confidentiality

Use appropriate confidentiality agreements where justified, limit access to sensitive material and maintain secure records of investor communications and data handling.

Manage conflicts and fairness

Identify and manage conflicts of interest. Handle investor enquiries consistently and obtain legal advice where disclosure, fundraising or financial-promotion rules may apply.

Close the feedback loop

Track substantive investor questions, provide considered responses and explain when a question cannot be answered or when further information may become available.

Review the relationship

Periodically assess whether communications are timely, accurate, useful and appropriately governed. Improve the process as the company and its investor base develop.
